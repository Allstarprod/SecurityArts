01 How it works

A receipt, not a verdict.

A seal is a certificate for one exact file. We fingerprint its bytes, sign the fingerprint, and publish the record, so anyone can check which account sealed it, and when.

Seal a piece → Verify a seal

02 Three steps

From finished file to public certificate.

AI detectors estimate a probability, and they can be wrong. A seal doesn't guess. It records which account sealed which file, and when, and anyone can check that record.

  1. You choose the finished file

    Pick the finished piece. We read the exact bytes of that file. Nothing in it is changed, and nothing is added on top of your image.

  2. We fingerprint and sign it

    We work out a SHA-256 fingerprint of the raw bytes and sign it with the SecurityArts key, using ECDSA P-256. Change a single pixel and the file no longer matches the fingerprint. That's what makes a seal tamper-evident.

  3. You get a public certificate

    The seal page shows the fingerprint, the signature, the key that signed it, the account that sealed it and the time we recorded. Anyone can check it without an account. It tells them which account sealed that exact file, and when. It doesn't tell them how the work was made.

03 The honest part

What a seal shows, and what it doesn't.

What a seal shows

  • Which SecurityArts account sealed this exact file
  • The time SecurityArts recorded the seal
  • That the bytes haven't changed since, which anyone can check

What it doesn't claim

  • It isn't an AI detector, and it never guesses
  • It doesn't prove a person made the work
  • It doesn't prove the work is original, or who owns it
  • It won't match a screenshot, or a resized, re-saved or re-encoded copy
  • It doesn't judge the art. It only records which account sealed what, and when

04 The certificate

What's on a certificate.

Every sealed piece gets a page of its own, at a link you can share. Here's what it records, line by line.

Two lines are easy to mix up. Sealed by is the account that sealed the file, and SecurityArts records it. Credited to is the name the sealer typed in. It's what they say, and SecurityArts doesn't check it.

The fingerprint belongs to the exact bytes that were sealed. A screenshot, or a copy that's been resized, re-saved or re-encoded, is a different set of bytes and won't match. Keep your original file: that's the one the certificate is about.

Sealed by
The SecurityArts account that sealed the file
Credited to
The name the sealer gave. We don't check it
Fingerprint
SHA-256 of the exact bytes
Signature
ECDSA P-256, made with the SecurityArts key
Signed with
The id of that key. It names our key, not the artist
Sealed
The time SecurityArts recorded

05 For the curious

Technical details.

For anyone who wants to check a seal without taking this page's word for it.

  • Hash. SHA-256 over the raw file bytes, exactly as uploaded. Nothing is re-encoded first, which is why a screenshot or re-saved copy gives a different hash and won't match.
  • Signature. ECDSA on curve P-256 (secp256r1) with SHA-256, made by the SecurityArts service key.
  • Signer. The signer id on every certificate identifies the SecurityArts service key, not the artist. Artists are identified by their account and don't hold a signing key.
  • Public key. GET /api/seal/pubkey returns the current signer id and public key.
  • Verify by hash. GET /api/verify/<sha256-hex> returns whether a sealed work with that hash exists, whether its signature verifies, the signer id, and the sealing timestamp.
  • One file, one record. Identical bytes can't be sealed twice.

The exact verification procedure is published alongside the public key. Questions the steps don't answer are in the FAQ.
